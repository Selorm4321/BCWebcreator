That is the honest answer to "how much does a website cost" — the number depends on the job, and anyone quoting you a single figure before hearing about your business is guessing. What you can hold me to is the process: a short call, one fixed price in writing, then the build.

What actually drives the price

Six things move the number, and they are the same six I ask about on the scope call:

1. How many pages

A one-page site for a new business is a different job from a ten-page site with separate service pages for each city you serve.

2. E-commerce, booking or members

Selling products, taking appointments, or gating content behind a login each add real engineering — payment flows, availability logic, and the testing that goes with them.

3. Whether copy is written or supplied

If you have your text ready, that is a straight build. If we are starting from a blank page, the words have to be written before they can be laid out.

4. Photography

Existing photos cost nothing. Sourcing or shooting them is a separate piece of work, and it changes the schedule more than it changes the build.

5. Integrations

Payments, calendars, CRM, email marketing. Each connection is small on its own and adds up when several are needed together.

6. What happens after launch

Some owners want to edit their own pages and be left alone. Others want a monthly hand on updates. That choice is priced separately and never bundled in silently.

How the price is built: hours first, then one number

Every project is estimated in hours before it is priced — design, build, content, testing and launch. That estimate becomes a single fixed price in writing. You are never billed by the hour, and you never see a timesheet.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Hourly billing rewards the person doing the work for being slow, and it charges the client for asking questions. A fixed price moves that risk onto me, where it belongs: if the build takes longer than I estimated, that is my problem, not your invoice.

A typical small business site is measured in tens of hours, not hundreds — the work is design, build, content, testing and launch on a small number of pages. A site with e-commerce or online booking runs longer, because payments, availability logic and testing all sit on top of the design work.

For context on where that leaves you: agencies in this region commonly bill $100 to $150 per hour, so the same forty hours of work is a $4,000–$6,000 invoice. A floor of $1,200 is possible here because there is no agency overhead — no account manager, no sales team, no office — not because the work is quicker or thinner.

What a fixed price includes

  • Design and build, including a mobile layout that is tested on a real phone
  • Basic SEO setup — page titles, meta descriptions, structured data, sitemap
  • Connecting your domain and hosting
  • Full ownership of the finished website, held by you, not by me

Copywriting, photography and paid advertising are quoted separately when needed. Ongoing maintenance is optional. Anything outside that is quoted as a separate item before it is built, never added silently at the end.

How long it takes

Most sites launch two to four weeks after the scope call. Simple sites with content ready land at the short end; e-commerce, booking systems, or large amounts of new copy push toward the longer end. The schedule is agreed with the fixed price, before work starts.
